What to carry into 2027

  1. Move IPSR and coaching to the start of the cycle and link them. Run them soon after awards and before the season, close together, with coaching briefed on the IPSR package. Timing was raised for coaching by — tracks and for IPSR by —; — said the two felt disconnected.
  2. Keep the external coach, change the format. Teams valued outside challenge but asked for preparation, field visits, an internal session before partners join, and follow-up over the year rather than a one-day visit. Delivery style strained partners in — tracks.
  3. Lighten and tailor IPSR. Fewer questions, scoring kept qualitative or back-end, categories adapted for partnership and systems innovations, and a refreshed facilitator pool. — found it heavy; — said the categories did not fit, and — found action-plan horizons too short.
  4. Fund the follow-through and match asks to budgets. Budget action-plan follow-up, stakeholder meetings and coaching logistics; consolidate S4I requests into one early calendar.
  5. Support adoption measurement over a multi-year horizon. — tracks shifted from reach to adoption this year; help with baselines and repeat-use metrics, and judge year one on foundations.
  6. Protect partner trust with earlier, clearer decisions. Share funding and selection results before the planning season and keep partners informed.

1 · Context & demand

How did you identify demand for your solution at the start, and what could have helped you improve demand orientation?

Almost every track built on years of earlier work rather than a fresh demand assessment, and most anchored demand in a government target or programme. Evidence was strongest where teams had their own surveys or trials. The weaker cases were those where demand had been assumed from partners' stated interest or from political signals, and where it differed sharply between user groups.

What would improve demand orientation

  • Test stated partner interest early, for example by whether partners show up and commit, before building the pathway on it (Digital Financial Services, Mechanisation Zambia).
  • Segment demand by region and user: new areas in Morocco needed a full package, and parents' ability to pay for school meals varied.
  • Check political feasibility up front where scaling uses state machinery (Balady bread).
  • Some teams said demand is not the constraint at all: investment is. Mapping larger investors to influence may matter more than proving demand (Forage).

2 · Plan: the IPSR process

IPSR was most valued as a convening device: it put actors who rarely meet in one structured conversation and produced owners and commitments. Teams with long histories said it rarely revealed new bottlenecks but helped them rank the ones they knew. The main criticisms were weight, fit and timing.

Answers to the guide questions

What key issues did the workshop bring that you had not considered?

Local machine dealers and block-level platforms (DSR-ZTW); regulatory mismatch between loan and insurance terms (DFS); early-generation forage seed shortages (Forage); role conflicts between millers and distributors and the need for digital seed tracing (Rice PPP); climate adaptation and a GESI action plan (Maize Nepal); separating seed and CA into distinct pathways (Morocco); rice-straw mulch access (PZTM); how much coordination matters (Solar irrigation); unused financial tools and land-tenure negotiation (Water & soil bundles).

Were the right people in the room, at the right time?

Mostly yes, with deliberate efforts to bring new actors (Nepal ran a pre-workshop for this). Gaps were commercial banks, rice importers, private forage seed companies, top-level government decision makers, farmers (Solar irrigation) and, in some Guatemalan workshops, the agriculture ministry and microfinance institutions. Several workshops came after the season had started.

Did the standard categories fit?

Less well for partnership and systems innovations such as PPPs, school-meal procurement and insurance-linked credit; one team found categories such as awareness, skills and capacity too general to separate without explanation. Readiness and use scoring was seen as a portfolio tool; one team heard officials ask whether it was for someone's PhD. Suggestions: tailor categories per track, co-design the workshop with the technical team, use IPSR to verify an existing pathway rather than restart from theory, and set action plans over one to three years rather than 30–90 days.

How did partners find it?

Partners appreciated the structure and the chance to hear each other, and some saw it as capacity building they could reuse; some found guidelines hard to follow and long question sets tiring, and one team adapted the five-whys step into a problem tree. Mature tracks used self-service or treated IPSR as a back-end tool, sometimes without partners knowing.

3 · Implement: evidence and partners

The clearest shift of the year was from counting reach to tracking adoption and repeat use, usually prompted by coaching. Many teams started without a baseline and rely on partners' own records. Several said adoption cannot honestly be shown within one season, and that political and relationship work is hard to capture in reporting systems.

Answers to the guide questions

How did you know whether you were doing the right things?

Mostly through field teams, partner meetings and WhatsApp groups. Formal change came after coaching: Mechanisation Zambia turned recommendations into an activity plan with trackers; Rice Transplanter began phoning past trainees to check adoption.

What could improve monitoring of the scaling pathway?

Baselines (several are only now being run), repeat-purchase and continued-use indicators, adoption-degree surveys, digital tracing of seed and services, and access agreements for private partners' sales records.

Did partners change what they were doing?

Yes in most tracks: governments gazetted guidelines and set up task forces, private firms invested in storage and onboarded to digital systems, seed actors took ownership of SOPs and grading, and partners recruited further partners.

4 · Adapt & analyse: coaching and the 2027 pitch

Coaching produced the most concrete strategic changes of any support element: narrower focus, separated pathways, new bottlenecks such as brood stock and early-generation seed, and a turn to adoption metrics. Criticism centred on timing, preparation, a one-off format, and a delivery style that some partners found harsh, though one team noticed it improved later in the year.

Answers to the guide questions

What did coaching bring up that you hadn't been asking?

Hatchery grading and brood-stock supply (Aquatic seed); degrees of adoption versus enrolment (PNM); focusing on one service model and one geography (Rice Transplanter); a self-propelling design with lead firms (Maize Nepal); the system stability needed at national scale (Agvisely); simple over complex content (Shamba Shape Up); separating CA components (Morocco); multiplier effects such as farmers acting as brokers (Solar irrigation); linking territorial partners into a national strategy (Water & soil bundles).

What would you change about coaching?

Hold it at the start, after awards and before the season; brief the coach with the proposal and IPSR package; use a structured question guide; visit the field; meet the CGIAR team before partners; follow up periodically; and integrate it into existing partner calls rather than adding meetings.

How did preparing the 2027 pitch help you take stock?

For — tracks it clarified a pivot or a clearer plan. For — it was a heavy and opaque process, arriving straight after coaching and stage-gating.

Going forward: responsible scaling

Inclusion is now planned more intentionally in — tracks (gender-neutral machine access, women seedling entrepreneurs, GESI action plans, women and youth bargaining power). — tracks plan sustainability through partner ownership, embedding in public systems or influencing larger investors. Trade-offs were rarely discussed explicitly.

5 · The support overall

Teams saw the elements as complementary in principle: IPSR to convene and diagnose, coaching to challenge and focus, the S4I team to explain and unblock. In practice they arrived too close together and too late in a short year, and the combined asks outweighed small budgets. The personal responsiveness of the coordination team was the most consistently praised element.

  3. Changes pushed by both went further. — of jointly pushed changes are done, against — for IPSR alone and — for coaching alone. The joint group is small, so read this as a signal rather than a finding.
  4. IPSR-led changes involved partners more often. Partners took part in — of IPSR-only changes and — of coaching-only changes.
  5. Coaching usually extended IPSR rather than repeating it. In — tracks coaching built beyond the IPSR findings, in — each added something different, and in — both pushed the same finding. Only — said coaching mostly confirmed IPSR, and both of those also said the coach had not built on the IPSR package.
  6. Teams agree on early, disagree on order. — tracks want both early in the cycle, before the season, and — want coaching to start from the IPSR package. On order, — suggested coaching first, so grassroots insights reach the workshop, while one mature track wanted coaching only after a season of results. In Bihar, where the two happened in the same week, both pointed to the same change, mulch hubs, which partners then took up.

A combined cycle for 2027

Drawn from what teams asked for; each step answers a problem raised in the conversations.

  1. Brief. Coach and IPSR facilitator read the proposal and any existing IPSR package, and meet the team to set context before anyone visits.
  2. Convene early. Hold the IPSR workshop soon after awards and before the season, with categories tailored to the track and a lighter question set.
  3. Coach within weeks, in the field. Start from the IPSR action plan, see sites and partners, and hold an internal CGIAR session before partners join.
  4. Agree one action plan with owners and money. Merge IPSR and coaching recommendations into a single plan, with a named owner and a budget line for each follow-up.
  5. Check in mid-season. A short follow-up on the joint plan, folded into the team's existing partner calls.
  6. Review after the season. Use results to update the package and feed the next pitch, rather than pitching straight after coaching.
